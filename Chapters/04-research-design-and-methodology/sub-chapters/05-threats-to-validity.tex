\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:methodology:threats}

Construct validity concerns the gap between what an instrument records and what the experiment intends to measure, and two outcomes are exposed to it. Network transfer is counted at the host boundary (Section~\ref{sec:methodology:metrics:primary}), so transport framing, encryption, and retransmission below that boundary are not separated out, and the metric approximates the bytes moved over the network rather than measuring them exactly. Because the same boundary applies to every configuration, this bears on absolute figures more than on comparisons. The k-nearest-neighbor workload raises a second concern: distance ordering is index-served only with a bounding-box predicate (Section~\ref{sec:database-engines}), so engines may rank by a bounding-box proxy before refining to exact distance, and fixing the query point and returned count bounds but does not remove the risk that two engines order candidates by different internal distances.

Internal validity asks whether an observed difference reflects the factor under test rather than a confound, and the dominant confound is the variability of shared cloud infrastructure. Production cloud services vary over time and between nominally identical allocations \parencite{iosup2011_performance_variability_cloud_services}, and instances of the same specification differ measurably in processor, input/output, and network throughput \parencite{schad2010_runtime_measurements_cloud}. The four measures of Section~\ref{sec:methodology:overview:replication} limit this rather than assume it away: concurrent pairing on identically provisioned instances, seeded randomization of execution order, warmup to steady state, and a fresh cluster per distributed run. Running test and control on one instance in randomized order is what lets a small difference read as an effect rather than as drift.

External validity concerns how far the findings extend beyond their conditions, and the design runs in a single region, with a single provider, on one dataset domain. Architectural orderings, which access pattern moves fewer bytes and whether distribution repays its coordination cost, are expected to carry beyond these conditions because they follow from format and engine structure (Section~\ref{sec:formats-summary}; Section~\ref{sec:distributed-and-non-distributed-compute}) rather than from the platform. Absolute times and costs do not, since they depend on provider- and region-specific rates whose predictability itself varies \parencite{leitner2016_patterns_chaos_iaas}. Data is the most limited axis: a workload governed by spatial skew (Section~\ref{subsec:partitioning}), such as the distributed join, is the most sensitive to building polygons not representing other inputs.

Three asymmetries are accepted by design rather than corrected, and each is recorded against the comparison it affects. Shapefile is read from local disk (Figure~\ref{fig:design-space}), lacking a cloud-native read path, which favors it on transfer, so a Shapefile transfer comparison is read as a lower bound rather than a like-for-like figure. The distributed configuration alone may cross a region boundary and so incur an egress charge the single-node configurations avoid (Section~\ref{sec:methodology:metrics:cost}). Warmup is omitted for it because each run provisions a fresh cluster, so its first-run variance is carried into the analysis rather than primed away (Section~\ref{sec:methodology:overview:replication}). None is hidden in an aggregate; each is reported against the workload and dimension it touches.
