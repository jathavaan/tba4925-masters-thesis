\section{Synthesis: which engine, and when}
\label{discussion:synthesis-which-engine-when}

Sections~\ref{discussion:single-node-cloud-native-vs-traditional} and~\ref{discussion:distribution-and-scaling} interpreted the single-machine and the distributed orderings separately. \acrshort{rq}3 requires reading them together, as a practitioner choosing a configuration for a given task must (Section~\ref{results:consistency-of-winners}). No configuration led on every dimension. PostGIS held the lowest run-time in every single-machine cell, and Apache Sedona held the best recorded national-join run-time from the medium tier upward. The operational-cost winner changed from cell to cell: DuckDB on two small-tier query patterns, the local Shapefile path on a third, PostGIS across the large single-machine cells, and Apache Sedona on the medium and large join (Figure~\ref{fig:winners-matrix}, Table~\ref{tab:geomean-normalized-performance}). The question of a single best engine is therefore ill-posed. The winner is fixed only once the workload pattern, the size tier, and the outcome dimension are set, and changing any one of them can change it.

This synthesis ranks the configurations on time and on cost alone. The host-boundary byte count is not a third axis of merit. It was read per architecture in Sections~\ref{discussion:single-node-cloud-native-vs-traditional} and~\ref{discussion:distribution-and-scaling}, where it located where each engine performs its work rather than scoring one configuration above another, and it is not re-scored here. Transfer enters the ranking only where it reaches operational cost, through the egress term of the cost model (Equation~\ref{eq:cost-model}), and the cost dimension already carries that contribution.

Time and cost do not order the configurations the same way. In the parallel-coordinates view the polylines cross between the time, cost, and mean-rank axes rather than running parallel, so a configuration that leads on one dimension trails on another (Figure~\ref{fig:outcome-parallel-coordinates}). Rank and magnitude diverge just as sharply: a near-flat rank line across the two ranked dimensions can sit over magnitudes that differ by more than two orders of magnitude on the same dimension (Figure~\ref{fig:rank-portability}), so moving one rank position is not a small movement in absolute time or cost. This split follows from how cloud resources are billed. Under per-resource metering each resource is charged on its own dimension rather than on elapsed time \parencite{mell2011_nist_definition_of_cloud_computing} (Section~\ref{background:cloud-native-data-architecture:cloud-cost-model}), so the resource a workload consumes, not how long it runs, sets its cost, and the fastest configuration need not be the cheapest. Apache Sedona has a further source of divergence: a cluster is billed per node-hour and carries the cost of the resources provisioned for it \parencite{databricks2024_cost_optimization_best_practices} (Section~\ref{background:distributed-spatial-processing:databricks-as-a-sedona-run-time}), so its position on cost reflects the cluster held rather than the run-time achieved.

The orderings are not equally portable across the size tiers. Configuration rank by run-time stays flat across the single-machine tiers: PostGIS held the fastest rank at both the small and the large tier on every query pattern. On the national join the ranking reorders (Figure~\ref{fig:ranking-stability}). PostGIS falls from first to third, Apache Sedona rises from second to first and holds that position at the large tier, and DuckDB moves from third to second between the small and the medium tier. The contrast follows from how much execution each workload exercises. A single-machine range query is answered by one engine reading an indexed, resident working set, whereas the national join runs the full lifecycle of partitioning, redistribution, execution, and collection, whose stages shift in relative weight with the size of the data and the size of the cluster \parencite{yu2019_geospark_perspective_beyond} (Section~\ref{background:distributed-spatial-processing:the-lifecycle-of-a-distributed-spatial-query}). The join is therefore the workload whose ordering can move as the tier grows. The point estimate that places Apache Sedona first from the medium tier should be read together with the test behind it: at the small and medium join tiers the leading configuration and its nearest competitor are read as statistically tied, so the synthesis names no run-time winner there, for the power reason set out in Section~\ref{discussion:reach-and-limits}.

The configurations these orderings rank occupy the flat-file, file-database, and full-database-engine tiers the background distinguishes (Section~\ref{background:spatial-data-formats-and-storage-models:summary}, Table~\ref{tab:format-summary}), together with the distributed tier read in Section~\ref{discussion:distribution-and-scaling}. Those tiers also rise in implementation and operational complexity, so the decision that follows can be read as a progression across them. At the low-complexity end sits the flat file reached through a library, the GeoPandas over Shapefile path, which carries no embedded engine and no native spatial index and so falls furthest behind on the index-dependent k-nearest-neighbor pattern (Section~\ref{discussion:single-node-cloud-native-vs-traditional}, Section~\ref{discussion:reach-and-limits}). The single-node and distributed orderings are governed by the same three properties. Table~\ref{tab:format-summary} compares: native spatial indexing, primary access pattern, and \acrshort{http} range-request support. Indexing capability set the single-node run-time, while the access pattern and its range-request reads produced the per-architecture transfer signature (Sections~\ref{discussion:single-node-cloud-native-vs-traditional} and~\ref{discussion:distribution-and-scaling}).

The decision the thesis frames weighs the implementation and operational complexity a configuration demands against the usage cost it incurs, and these patterns map onto it. For single-machine analytical queries, a tuned single-node PostGIS is the configuration to reach for. It held the fastest run-time on every single-machine pattern and tier, and its rank did not move across tiers (Figure~\ref{fig:winners-matrix}, Figure~\ref{fig:ranking-stability}). That lead tracks the native spatial index PostGIS exposes rather than the engine in the abstract; it holds for the native deployments compared and is subject to the index-and-locality confound (Section~\ref{discussion:single-node-cloud-native-vs-traditional}). Within the tested range it is therefore the lead most likely to hold as scale changes. Distribution earns its added operational complexity once the working set outgrows a single machine. At the large join tier neither single-node engine completed the workload while the broadcast join did, a difference of feasibility rather than of speed (Section~\ref{discussion:distribution-and-scaling}). Where exactly that threshold falls is bounded by the tested tiers, since the single-node engines completed the medium tier and failed only at the large one. The small-tier picture runs the other way. Within the \numrange{2}{16} workers tested the broadcast join never overtook the PostGIS minimum, and the Results chapter declared no winner at the largest cluster. Any crossover lies beyond the tested worker range and is an extrapolation rather than a measured point (Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:statistical-analysis:answering-rq2-scaling-against-single-node}).

DuckDB sits on a different axis of the decision, where its value is operational rather than a matter of latency. It lost to PostGIS on single-machine run-time by orders of magnitude, yet it recorded the lowest operational cost on two of the small-tier query patterns, and cost is a ranked dimension of the comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:winners-matrix}). Its read footprint is the other operational property worth naming: DuckDB received a near-constant payload whatever the number of rows a query returned, a predictable, cardinality-independent signature of range-request access with column and row-group pruning, established per architecture in Section~\ref{discussion:single-node-cloud-native-vs-traditional} (Figure~\ref{fig:bytes-vs-cardinality}). That footprint records how the format is read, not a transfer advantage to be ranked. At the single-node scale tested it carried no cost benefit: the egress term of the cost model was zero for every single-node configuration, so the bytes received never reached operational cost (Equation~\ref{eq:cost-model}, Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:metrics-and-cost-model:cost-model}). Such a footprint becomes an operational advantage only in a setting these measurements do not reach, one that bills cross-region egress or scans enough input to move the compute term. Across the decision, the configurations the tests separated carry a winner and those they did not are read as either choice, and any boundary drawn beyond the tested tiers or worker counts is an extrapolation rather than a measured line.
