\section{Orchestration and pairing}
\label{system-architecture-and-implementation:orchestration-and-pairing}

The per-configuration implementations described so far execute under a single orchestrator, \texttt{main.py}, whose role is to realize the pairing protocol of Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:overview-and-experimental-design:replication-and-randomization}. Its control flow operates at two levels: a serial outer pass admits one batch of paired configurations at a time, and a parallel inner step runs that batch's members together as \acrshort{aci} container groups, so paired configurations share a single wall-clock window. The subsections that follow specify the dispatch loop, the constraints a batch must satisfy, and the resulting batch listing.

\subsection{Dispatch loop and batch graph}
\label{system-architecture-and-implementation:orchestration-and-pairing:dispatch-loop-and-batch-graph}

The orchestrator in \texttt{main.py} drives the suite through a single serial loop, so that only one batch of experiments is ever in flight. It walks the experiment list in order, skips any entry already recorded in \texttt{completed\_experiments}, and treats the next remaining entry as the seed of a batch. The members of that batch are the experiments named in the seed's \texttt{related\_script\_ids} list, which every peer declares reciprocally, so the union of these reciprocal declarations forms the batch graph. The seed and its peers launch together across a thread pool capped at ten workers (\texttt{ThreadPoolExecutor} with \texttt{max\_workers=10}), one Azure Container Instance per member; the loop blocks until the whole batch has finished, marks every member completed, and only then advances. Because each member is appended to \texttt{completed\_experiments} once its batch returns, the reciprocal declarations are de-duplicated and each batch executes exactly once, independent of which member the outer loop reached first. Running the peers of a batch within one wall-clock window is what makes their measurements comparable, for the reasons established in Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:overview-and-experimental-design:replication-and-randomization}. Figure \ref{fig:dispatch-loop} traces this control flow, from the serial admission of one batch through the parallel launch of its members in their own container groups to the join barrier on which the loop blocks before marking the batch completed.

Failure is contained at the level of the individual member rather than the batch. Each member runs inside \texttt{\_safe\_run}, which catches any exception raised while launching or monitoring that container, records the failed identifier, and lets the remaining peers run to completion instead of aborting the batch. After the batch returns, the orchestrator inspects the collected failures and logs whether the batch failed in part or in full, noting that surviving peers ran without their matched counterparts and that the fair-comparison assumption may not hold for that batch; the warning lets such batches be filtered out during analysis. Every member executes in its own container group provisioned with \texttt{restart-policy Never}, so a member that exits is never silently retried on the same instance and each measurement reflects a single clean execution.

\subsection{Batching constraints}
\label{system-architecture-and-implementation:orchestration-and-pairing:batching-constraints}

Co-scheduling peers in one wall-clock window yields a fair comparison only when those peers do not perturb one another as they run, so the batch graph admits a group of experiments only when four conditions hold at once. The first two conditions keep every batch internally comparable. A batch is restricted to a single query type, so that no comparison spans a mismatch in workload, and to a single dataset-size tier, so that storage-cache state and the regional load on shared virtual-machine capacity remain comparable across the group rather than confounded by a size mismatch.

The remaining two conditions cap contention on infrastructure that the members genuinely share. At most one PostGIS experiment may join a batch, because the managed PostgreSQL Flexible Server is a single shared instance and two PostGIS queries executing against it concurrently would contend for its shared buffers, operating-system page cache, and \acrshort{cpu}. The Databricks clusters impose the second shared limit. Each Sedona member provisions a cluster of \texttt{Standard\_D4s\_v3} nodes at four \acrshort{vcpu} each, so a cluster of $w$ workers consumes $(w + 1) \times 4$ \acrshort{vcpu} once its driver is included, and the sum over a batch must remain within the workspace's regional quota of 200 \acrshort{vcpu} for that node family. DuckDB, Shapefile, and PostGIS draw on a separate \acrshort{aci} quota and do not count toward this cap. Because DuckDB and Shapefile run process-local inside their own container groups and each Sedona variant provisions its own cluster on disjoint virtual machines, co-scheduled members share no runtime state beyond the regional \acrshort{vcpu} pool that the fourth condition already bounds. A candidate batch is therefore admissible only when all four conditions hold simultaneously, and the assignment of experiments to batches that satisfies them is listed in Section~\ref{system-architecture-and-implementation:orchestration-and-pairing:batch-listing}.
