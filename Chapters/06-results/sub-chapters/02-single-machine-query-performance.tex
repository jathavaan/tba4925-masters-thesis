\section{Single-machine query performance (RQ1)}
\label{results:single-machine-query-performance}

The single-machine results cover three single-node configurations: DuckDB over GeoParquet, PostGIS, and GeoPandas over Shapefile, the last exercised at the small tier only, so its larger-tier cells are absent by design rather than failed. DuckDB and PostGIS ran the point-in-polygon lookup, the k-nearest-neighbor search, and the bounding-box filter at both the small and large tiers, and the national-scale join enters where the single-node engines executed it. The three configurations are reported in turn across the data transferred over the network, the wall-clock time of each query, and the operational cost charged for it.

\subsection{Data transferred}
\label{results:single-machine-query-performance:data-transferred}

Figure~\ref{fig:single-machine-bytes-transferred} shows the network bytes received by each configuration across the workload-by-tier grid, on a logarithmic axis. The local Shapefile path reads from disk, so its received volume is approximately zero, at \SI{0}{\byte} for the point-in-polygon and bounding-box queries and \SI{42}{\byte} for the k-nearest-neighbor search. DuckDB over object storage received a near-constant payload of \SI{93323}{\byte} at the small tier across all three patterns, falling to approximately \SI{12000}{\byte} at the large tier. For the point-in-polygon lookup and the k-nearest-neighbor search DuckDB received the most of the three configurations, whereas for the bounding-box filter PostGIS received the most, reaching \SI{1703441}{\byte} at the small tier and \SI{1858684}{\byte} (approximately \SI{1.9}{\mega\byte}) at the large tier; PostGIS otherwise stayed low, at \SI{1923}{\byte} for the point-in-polygon lookup and \SI{8688}{\byte} and \SI{7927}{\byte} for the k-nearest-neighbor search. The cells separated by several orders of magnitude on the logarithmic axis. Each query returned a deterministic payload, and the marked median estimator and its \num{95}\% bootstrap confidence interval coincide to negligible width.

Figure~\ref{fig:single-machine-bytes-directional} separates the two directions. For both DuckDB and PostGIS the bytes received exceeded the bytes sent by orders of magnitude: the sent direction was sub-kilobyte request traffic in most cells, around \SIrange{600}{900}{\byte}, rising only for the PostGIS bounding-box filter, which sent \SI{13648}{\byte} at the small tier and \SI{14770}{\byte} at the large tier. The local Shapefile path moved only tens of bytes in either direction and is marked as not like-for-like, reading from the local filesystem rather than across a client boundary.

The received volumes relate to the number of result rows each query returned, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bytes-vs-cardinality}. The point-in-polygon lookup returned a single row, the k-nearest-neighbor search \num{10} rows, the national-scale join \num{357} rows, and the bounding-box filter several thousand, at \numrange{2682}{3015} rows depending on engine and tier. DuckDB's received volume varied little with cardinality, so its per-row transfer fell as the result grew, whereas the PostGIS received volume rose with the row count. The returned counts agreed across the engines that ran each workload, as Table~\ref{tab:appendix-cardinality-agreement} reports. \subsection{Wall-clock time}
\label{results:single-machine-query-performance:wall-clock-time}

Figure~\ref{fig:single-machine-wall-clock-time} shows the wall-clock time of each configuration, summarized by the minimum estimator on a logarithmic axis. PostGIS recorded the lowest run-time in every single-machine cell, ranging from approximately \SI{0.0015}{\second} for the point-in-polygon lookup and the k-nearest-neighbor search to \SI{0.0389}{\second} for the bounding-box filter at the large tier. DuckDB ran several orders of magnitude slower, from \SI{1.327}{\second} for the point-in-polygon lookup at the small tier to \SI{29.919}{\second} at the large tier, with the k-nearest-neighbor search and the bounding-box filter between those bounds. The local Shapefile path fell between the two engines for the point-in-polygon lookup, at \SI{0.3427}{\second}, and the bounding-box filter, at \SI{0.0521}{\second}, at the small tier; for the k-nearest-neighbor search, however, it was the slowest configuration measured, at \SI{47.816}{\second} against DuckDB's \SI{0.7460}{\second}. The minimum-estimator run-times and their bootstrap confidence-interval half-widths appear in Table~\ref{tab:results-achieved-n}.

Figure~\ref{fig:single-machine-latency-ecdf} shows the per-iteration latency distribution for the point-in-polygon lookup at the small tier, the one cell in which all three configurations ran. The three curves are separated by orders of magnitude across the whole distribution. PostGIS stayed in the single-digit-millisecond range, from a median ($p_{50}$) of \SI{0.0037}{\second} through \SI{0.0068}{\second} at $p_{95}$ to \SI{0.0105}{\second} at $p_{99}$; the local Shapefile path ran from $p_{50}$ \SI{0.3741}{\second} to $p_{99}$ \SI{0.6133}{\second}; and DuckDB from $p_{50}$ \SI{1.5252}{\second} to $p_{99}$ \SI{3.3482}{\second}. The median-to-tail spread was widest for PostGIS, whose $p_{99}$ was about \num{2.8} times its median, and tightest for the local path at about \num{1.6} times, with DuckDB between at roughly \num{2.2} times.

\subsection{Operational cost}
\label{results:single-machine-query-performance:operational-cost}

Figure~\ref{fig:single-machine-operational-cost} decomposes the operational cost of each configuration into the four terms of the cost model defined in Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:metrics-and-cost-model:cost-model}: compute, storage, operations, and egress. At these data sizes the compute term dominated the stack for both single-node engines, with storage a thin sliver, the operations term near zero and nonzero only for DuckDB at no more than \num{0.0023}~USD per workload, and the egress term zero throughout, every single-machine configuration staying within one region. The local Shapefile path carried the compute term alone, with zero storage, operations, and egress.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{Chapters/06-results/sub-chapters/figures/06-single-machine-operational-cost.pdf}
    \caption[Operational cost per single-machine configuration]{Operational cost of each single-machine configuration, faceted by workload pattern. Each bar is stacked into the four terms of the cost model (compute, storage, operations, and egress), whose definitions and unit prices are given in Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:metrics-and-cost-model:cost-model} and Table~\ref{tab:cost-model-unit-prices}; each configuration is charged only for the terms it consumes, so the local-filesystem Shapefile path carries the compute term alone.}
    \label{fig:single-machine-operational-cost}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:single-machine-cost-time-quadrant} places each configuration in the cost-time plane, one panel per workload pattern, with per-panel median cross-hairs marking the four decision quadrants. DuckDB at the large tier fell in the slow-and-costly quadrant in every panel in which it appeared, while the small-tier engines sat toward the cheaper and faster side; PostGIS, the fastest in absolute terms, sat at the low-time edge near the cost median in the point-in-polygon, k-nearest-neighbor, and bounding-box panels. In the national-scale join panel the two tiers separated, with both engines at the small tier in the fast-and-cheap quadrant and both at the medium tier in the slow-and-costly quadrant. The best-worker Sedona point appears in that panel alone, marked as a star at low time and moderate cost for context with the single-machine engines.
