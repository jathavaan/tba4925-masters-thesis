\section{Consistency of winners (RQ3)}
\label{results:consistency-of-winners}

The preceding sections reported single-machine query performance (Section~\ref{results:single-machine-query-performance}) and the distributed national-scale join (Section~\ref{results:distributed-join-and-scaling}) on their own terms. \acrshort{rq}3 reads those outputs together rather than collecting new measurements (Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:statistical-analysis:answering-rq3-consistency-across-patterns}), asking which configuration records the best value in each combination of workload pattern and outcome dimension, how separable the resulting rankings are, and how large the differences between configurations are. The readings that follow report the winners, the rank orderings across tiers and dimensions, and the points where one ordering inverts another; interpretation of these patterns is deferred.

% Run academic rewrite
Network transfer is examined for the consistency of its directional signature across the configurations rather than ranked across them. The host-boundary byte count is not a single commensurable quantity: it denotes the input scanned from object storage for the remote-read configurations (DuckDB and Apache Sedona), the returned result set for PostGIS, and near-zero local file-system transfer for the local Shapefile path, so the configurations cannot be ordered on one common byte axis. The cross-configuration winner ranking is therefore reported on wall-clock time and operational cost only, while the directional reading of network transfer is retained as a per-architecture characterization (Section~\ref{results:single-machine-query-performance}, Section~\ref{results:distributed-join-and-scaling}).

\subsection{Winners across dimensions}
\label{results:consistency-of-winners:winners-across-dimensions}

% Run academic rewrite
Figure~\ref{fig:winners-matrix} records the winning configuration in every workload pattern and size tier on each of the two ranked outcome dimensions, where the winner is the configuration with the best value among those that ran the cell. On wall-clock time, PostGIS recorded the lowest run-time in every single-machine cell across both tiers, while on the national-scale join Apache Sedona held the lowest run-time at the medium and large tiers. On operational cost the winner was mixed: DuckDB for the bounding-box filter and the k-nearest-neighbor search at the small tier, the local Shapefile path for the small-tier point-in-polygon lookup, PostGIS across the large single-machine cells, and Apache Sedona at the medium and large join. Network transfer is not ranked across the configurations, the host-boundary byte count not being commensurable across them; its directional signature is read instead from the per-architecture transfer figures (Figure~\ref{fig:single-machine-bytes-transferred}), where the local Shapefile path's transfer measured at approximately zero. The national join small and medium tiers do not yield a clear winner on every ranked dimension: on run-time the leading configuration was not statistically separated from its nearest competitor under the Holm-adjusted pairwise tests (Table~\ref{tab:appendix-pairwise-statistics}), so that entry is read as statistically tied rather than won.

\subsection{Ranking stability}
\label{results:consistency-of-winners:ranking-stability}

Figure~\ref{fig:ranking-stability} traces configuration rank by run-time as the size tier changes, with one line per configuration faceted by workload pattern and rank \num{1} marking the fastest. Across the three single-machine patterns the lines stay flat: PostGIS held rank \num{1} at both the small and the large tier for the bounding-box filter, the k-nearest-neighbor search, and the point-in-polygon lookup, with DuckDB second at the large tier in each. The national-scale join behaves differently. Between the small and the medium tier the lines cross: PostGIS falls from rank \num{1} to rank \num{3}, Apache Sedona rises from rank \num{2} to rank \num{1} and holds that position at the large tier, and DuckDB moves from rank \num{3} to rank \num{2}. The join therefore shows a rank inversion across tiers that the single-machine patterns do not.

\subsection{Statistical separability}
\label{results:consistency-of-winners:statistical-separability}

The separability of these rankings was tested where the design allowed it. A Friedman omnibus blocking by workload and tier with the configurations as treatments returned $\chi^2(2) = \num{4.67}$ at $p = \num{0.097}$ over the three single-machine configurations and the three small-tier cells that form a complete block; the national-scale join and Apache Sedona fall outside it, since Sedona ran the join alone and no complete three-treatment block spans them with the single-machine engines. With three blocks the omnibus carries little power, and the difference among the three configurations was not significant at the \num{0.05} level. Figure~\ref{fig:pairwise-significance-cliques} places the same three single-machine configurations on a mean-rank axis, PostGIS at \num{1.00}, the local Shapefile path at \num{2.33}, and DuckDB at \num{2.67}. No connector joins them: every pairwise difference was significant under the Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon tests (Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:statistical-analysis:pairwise-comparison-and-effect-size}), so no non-significance clique forms.

Figure~\ref{fig:vargha-delaney-forest} reports the Vargha--Delaney $\hat{A}_{12}$ effect size for the comparisons that anchor the single-node and distributed questions: DuckDB against PostGIS across the query cells, and Apache Sedona at eight nodes against DuckDB and PostGIS on the national join. Every marker sits at an extreme of the axis, at \num{0.0} or \num{1.0}, beyond the large-effect thresholds on either side of the \num{0.5} no-effect line (Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:statistical-analysis:pairwise-comparison-and-effect-size}), so wherever a difference exists one distribution separates completely from the other. Effect magnitude and statistical significance do not always coincide here: at the national join small and medium tiers the run-time and bytes differences carry these large effect sizes yet were not significant under the Holm adjustment, the ties noted above. The full set of pairwise tests, with the test statistic, the adjusted p-value, and the effect size for every comparison, is collected in Table~\ref{tab:appendix-pairwise-statistics}.

Read across the two ranked dimensions and the size tiers, the orderings held in some places and inverted in others. PostGIS held the fastest run-time in every single-machine cell and at both single-machine tiers, and the three single-machine configurations separated cleanly from one another on run-time. The orderings diverged on cost and on the join: the cost winner changed from cell to cell, the local Shapefile path trailed on time by more than two orders of magnitude, and the national-scale join inverted its run-time ranking between the small and the medium tier, with Apache Sedona taking the lead from the medium tier onward, the join small and medium cells remaining statistically tied on run-time. What these consistencies and inversions imply is taken up in the discussion.
