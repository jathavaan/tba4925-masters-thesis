\section{Distributed join and scaling (RQ2)}
\label{results:distributed-join-and-scaling}

The national-scale spatial join moved the comparison onto the distributed engine. It was run on Apache Sedona on Databricks across worker counts of \num{2}, \num{4}, \num{8}, \num{12}, and \num{16} nodes under two execution strategies, a broadcast join and a partitioned join, and measured against the single-node PostGIS and DuckDB engines at the small, medium, and large tiers. The scaling of the join is read first, then its absolute run-time against the single-node baselines, the decomposition of its execution into phases, and finally its operational cost and the cost-time tradeoff.

\subsection{Scaling behavior}
\label{results:distributed-join-and-scaling:scaling-behavior}

Figure~\ref{fig:distributed-speedup-efficiency} shows how the broadcast join scaled relative to the two-worker baseline of Equation~\ref{eq:speedup-parallel-efficiency}, with elapsed times taken under the minimum estimator of Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:statistical-analysis:summary-statistics-and-the-choice-of-estimator}. Speedup rose monotonically with worker count at every tier but stayed below the ideal-linear reference, and the shortfall widened as workers were added. At \num{16} workers the broadcast join reached a speedup of about \num{2.3} at the small tier, \num{5.3} at the medium tier, and \num{6.5} at the large tier, against the ideal of \num{8}, so the achieved speedup improved with tier. The paired parallel efficiency declined from one as workers were added: the decline was steepest at the small tier, where efficiency fell to about \num{0.29} at \num{16} workers, and gentlest at the large tier, where it remained near \num{0.81} and varied non-monotonically, dipping to about \num{0.79} at \num{8} workers before partially recovering. The partitioned series, drawn at the small tier only, stayed near unit speedup across the range, its efficiency falling to about \num{0.13} at \num{16} workers.

The two strategies differed by a wide margin at the small tier, the only tier at which the partitioned join completed. Figure~\ref{fig:distributed-strategy-contrast} contrasts them across worker counts. The partitioned join ran between about \SI{2540}{\second} and \SI{2760}{\second} across the worker counts, roughly two orders of magnitude slower than the broadcast join, which ran between about \SI{14}{\second} and \SI{32}{\second}. The same separation appeared in operational cost: the partitioned runs cost between about \num{11.9} and \num{56.8} US dollars, against about \num{0.38} to \num{1.59} dollars for broadcast, roughly \num{30} times as much. The partitioned medium- and large-tier runs did not complete, recording an executor out-of-memory failure at every worker count, as listed in Table~\ref{tab:distributed-join-descriptive-statistics}.

\subsection{Absolute performance vs. single-node baselines}
\label{results:distributed-join-and-scaling:absolute-performance-vs-single-node-baselines}

% NOTE: the small-tier PostGIS-vs-Sedona comparison below is reported neutrally
% (no winner declared) because no rank-sum / Mann-Whitney / Vargha-Delaney A12 test
% is currently wired into the RQ2 analysis path, and this section carries no p-value
% float. A pairwise test on these two samples should be obtainable from the persisted
% per-iteration data; wire it in and report significance and effect size here if/when
% available.
Figure~\ref{fig:distributed-wall-clock-vs-workers} places the distributed broadcast join against the single-node baselines at each tier, where the worker count at which a curve first drops below a baseline locates the crossover of Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:statistical-analysis:answering-rq2-scaling-against-single-node}. At the small tier the fastest single-node engine was PostGIS, at \SI{13.365}{\second}. The broadcast join fell from \SI{31.981}{\second} at \num{2} workers to \SI{13.747}{\second} at \num{16} workers (\num{95}\% bootstrap confidence-interval half-width \SI{0.486}{\second}), so it approached but did not drop below the PostGIS baseline within the tested range of \numrange{2}{16} workers. At the medium tier the fastest single-node engine was DuckDB, at about \SI{1386}{\second}; the broadcast join was already far below it at \num{2} workers, at \SI{203.4}{\second} (confidence-interval half-width \SI{1.010}{\second}), and fell to \SI{38.2}{\second} at \num{16} workers, so the distributed join led at every tested worker count. At the large tier neither single-node engine completed the join, so no baseline is plotted and only the distributed curve appears.

\subsection{Execution-phase breakdown}
\label{results:distributed-join-and-scaling:execution-phase-breakdown}

Figure~\ref{fig:distributed-execution-phase-breakdown} decomposes the broadcast join into the phase metrics of Section~\ref{research-design-and-methodology:metrics-and-cost-model:distributed-phase-metrics}. Panel~(a) shows that the executor-run phase accounted for effectively the whole of the stacked phase time at every tier and worker count, while driver collection contributed a negligible sliver, at most about \SI{2.2}{\second} and recorded as zero at several worker counts. The executor-run totals grew with tier, from a few hundred seconds at the small tier to several thousand seconds at the large tier, and did not fall as workers were added. Panel~(b) shows the shuffle volume, where the bytes read and the bytes written were nearly equal at every point. That volume grew with tier, from roughly \SIrange{0.5}{1}{\giga\byte} at the small tier to about \SIrange{5}{11}{\giga\byte} at the medium tier and \SIrange{13}{19}{\giga\byte} at the large tier, and varied only modestly with worker count within a tier.

At the driver boundary the returned result was small in absolute terms. Figure~\ref{fig:distributed-client-boundary} shows the directional transfer at the Sedona client boundary for the eight-worker broadcast configuration, with the median estimator marked on each direction. Bytes received ranged from about \SI{34}{\kilo\byte} at the small tier to about \SI{62}{\kilo\byte} at the large tier, and bytes sent from about \SI{18}{\kilo\byte} to about \SI{32}{\kilo\byte}, so received exceeded sent by roughly a factor of two at each tier, and both directions grew modestly with tier. This boundary is distinct from the shuffle volume of the phase breakdown, and is the distributed counterpart of the single-machine directional transfer in Figure~\ref{fig:single-machine-bytes-directional}.

\subsection{Operational cost and the cost-time tradeoff}
\label{results:distributed-join-and-scaling:operational-cost-and-the-cost-time-tradeoff}

Figure~\ref{fig:distributed-cost-time-pareto} places every strategy, tier, and worker count in the cost-time plane and traces the non-dominated frontier. The frontier was composed entirely of small-tier broadcast points, at \num{2}, \num{4}, \num{12}, and \num{16} workers; the small-tier broadcast point at \num{8} workers was dominated, as were all of the medium- and large-tier broadcast points and every partitioned point, each of which sat further up and to the right at both higher time and higher cost. The per-cell achieved iteration counts, minimum-estimator run-times, confidence-interval half-widths, and costs behind these figures are collected in Table~\ref{tab:distributed-join-descriptive-statistics}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Chapters/06-results/sub-chapters/figures/06-distributed-cost-time-pareto.pdf}
    \caption[Cost-time tradeoff of the distributed join]{Operational cost (USD) against wall-clock time (s) for the distributed national-scale spatial join, on logarithmic axes, with one point per join strategy, size tier, and worker count and each point annotated with its worker count. Line color encodes the join strategy, and the connected line traces the non-dominated (Pareto) frontier, the set of points not beaten on both cost and time by any other point.}
    \label{fig:distributed-cost-time-pareto}
\end{figure}
